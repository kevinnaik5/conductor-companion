The challenge

Issue tickets standing in a jerky bus, with cash in one hand.

Conductors still fight heavy keypad machines with worn buttons and unreadable screens. The brief: design the ticketing flow for a handheld touchscreen, and show how every action stays within reach.

What they do today

Switch to cash when UPI is slow, check payments by eye, push for exact change, and in some depots refuse UPI at peak.

What that tells me

Any feature that slows them at peak gets dropped. Any unclear money state gets avoided. Speed and certainty win.

What the research told me

Four insights shaped every decision.

01

Conductors work from memory and touch

Fixed positions must replace the physical keys they press without looking.

02

Anything slow at peak gets abandoned

UPI was dropped this way. The conductor must never wait on the machine.

03

The conductor pays for the machine's mistakes

Every money state must be unambiguous, and every mistake reversible.

04

DTC needs every passenger's exit stop

For route planning. A fare alone isn't enough — even for free pink-card riders.

"The conductor should never wait on the machine, and never pay for its mistakes."

Design principles

Seven rules, each answering a constraint.

grid_view

Fixed positions

Nothing moves between states. The thumb learns the layout.

restart_alt

Reset every ticket

Cash, adult, 1 passenger. No leftover settings after a jolt.

undo

Undo, not confirm

The last tap is the confirmation. Mistakes are reversed after.

my_location

Conductor over GPS

GPS suggests; its doubt is visible; one tap corrects it.

contrast

Colour is a 2nd signal

Every state also has an icon, a word and a place.

savings

Nothing charged early

Cancelling is always safe until the last tap.

do_not_touch

No gestures

A jolt can fake a swipe. Only taps on big, fixed targets.

flag

The reframe

Don't beat the one-press ticket. Win on destination, stops, payment and mistakes.

Key decision 1 · Choosing the destination

Tap the fare, then pick from only its stops.

Always two taps for a cash ticket. No cycling, no long scrolling, and the exact exit stop DTC needs.

Key decision 4 · Cards

A card knows how to pay — not where you're going.

Key decision 5 · Mistakes without pop-ups

No "Are you sure?" Just a way back.

After a few hundred tickets, a confirmation becomes a reflex tap. In a moving bus, a pop-up over the buttons causes the very mis-taps it should prevent.

Navigation and reachability

Every tap on every ticket is in the bottom 45%.

Designed for the right thumb of a hand that is also holding the device. Nothing reflows between states, so positions become muscle memory.

Read zone — route, journey box. Glanced at, rarely tapped.
Stretch zone — UPI bar, ticket strip, GPS answers. Occasional taps.
Thumb zone — payment, passengers, fares, stop list. Every ticket happens here.
The stop list opens directly above the fare just tapped, so the second tap is a short move up from the same thumb position. GPS answers moved from the top of the screen to the thumb zone during iteration.

Reflection and next steps

What I'd do next

directions_bus
Ride with conductors. Time the three-passenger scenario on a moving bus, in daylight.
translate
Hindi review by native speakers, ideally conductors themselves.
fact_check
Real data: 2026 fares, child fares, Route 548 stops and DTC stop codes.
help
Open questions: printing while the next ticket starts; a pink card tapped on another screen; 5+ passengers.

What I learned

The hardest constraint wasn't the four seconds — it was that the old machine already wins on its simplest ticket. Accepting that, and designing for everything around the tap, is what made the rest of the decisions clear.
